The Late-Payment Email Sequence: 3 Templates That Get You Paid Without Burning the Relationship

Late payment is the norm in freelance work, not the exception. In the Leapers annual survey of more than 1,000 freelancers (reported by Design Week), 68% said they had experienced late payment, and 43% had been presented with payment terms of 60 days or more. The Smallpdf Freelancer Freedom Index 2026 (a survey of 397 freelancers) found the same pattern at the shorter end: 54% wait more than a week for payment after invoicing, and 24% wait more than two weeks.

So an overdue invoice usually is not a crisis — but it does need a process. The mistake is writing each reminder from scratch, in the mood of the moment: too soft the first time, too sharp the third. A fixed sequence solves that. Three emails, sent at set intervals after the due date, each one complete and ready to copy: gentle at 7 days, clear at 14, final at 30. Because the tone is decided in advance, you can be firm without being personal — you are following a procedure, and the client can tell.

Before you send anything: the 2-minute checklist

A surprising share of "late payments" are paperwork problems on the sender's side. Run through this list before the first reminder:

  • Re-attach the invoice. Do not make the client search their inbox for it. Attach the original invoice to every reminder.
  • Check the recipient. Was the invoice sent to the person who actually pays — or to your day-to-day contact, who may never have forwarded it? If you are unsure, ask your contact who handles payment and copy that person in.
  • Check the amount matches. Confirm the invoice total matches the agreed fee, including any approved change requests, and that the invoice number, due date, and your payment details are correct and unchanged.
  • Check it was received. If the client says they never saw the invoice, that is a delivery problem, not a payment problem — resend and restart the clock from confirmed receipt.

Email 1 — Day 7 past due: the gentle check-in

Send this one week after the due date. The working assumption is an oversight: the invoice is sitting in an approvals queue or the wrong inbox. Keep it short, friendly, and easy to act on. Many invoices are paid at this stage with no friction at all.

Subject: Invoice [#1042] — just checking in

Hi [Name],

I hope you're well. I'm writing to check in on invoice [#1042]
for [amount], which was due on [date]. I haven't seen the payment
come through yet, so I wanted to make sure the invoice reached
the right person.

I've attached a copy in case it's helpful. If it's already in
your payment run, please ignore this — and thank you.

If anything looks off, or you need any details from me to process
it, just let me know.

Best regards,
[Your name]

Email 2 — Day 14 past due: clear and specific

Two weeks past due, drop the assumption that it might already be handled. State the facts — invoice number, amount, due date, days overdue — name a date by which you need payment or a confirmed payment date, and ask directly if there is a problem. The tone stays professional; the vagueness goes.

Subject: Invoice [#1042] now 14 days overdue — [amount]

Hi [Name],

I'm following up on invoice [#1042] for [amount], due on [date].
It's now 14 days past the due date, and I haven't received
payment or a confirmed payment date yet.

Could you please arrange payment by [date, e.g., Friday], or let
me know the exact date it will be paid? If there's an issue with
the invoice itself, tell me what it is and I'll fix it the same
day.

I've attached the invoice again for convenience. Thank you for
getting this sorted.

Best regards,
[Your name]

Email 3 — Day 30 past due: the final notice

At 30 days, the email changes job: it is no longer a reminder, it is a record. It restates the history (invoice, due date, previous reminders), sets a final deadline, and states the consequences you are actually prepared to carry out — pausing ongoing work is the standard one for freelancers. If your contract includes a late fee, this is where you refer to it. Only mention consequences you will genuinely act on; an empty threat teaches the client to ignore the next email too.

Subject: Final notice: invoice [#1042], [amount], 30 days overdue

Hi [Name],

This is my third follow-up regarding invoice [#1042] for
[amount], which was due on [date] and is now 30 days overdue.
I previously wrote on [date of email 1] and [date of email 2].

Please arrange payment by [final date, e.g., 7 days from today].
If payment isn't received by then, I'll need to pause all
ongoing work on [project/account] until the balance is cleared.
[If your agreement includes a late fee, add: As set out in our
agreement, a late fee of [amount or percentage] applies to
invoices overdue by more than 30 days and will be added to the
outstanding balance.]

If there's a problem I'm not aware of, I'd rather hear about it
now so we can agree a way forward. Otherwise, I look forward to
receiving payment by [final date].

Regards,
[Your name]

If the final deadline passes with no payment and no reply, the next step is outside email: a formal demand letter, a collections route, or legal advice, depending on the amount and your jurisdiction. That is a decision to make with the numbers in front of you — which is one more reason the written sequence above matters: it documents that you asked, clearly and repeatedly, before escalating.

Prevention: the terms that stop most late payment

Reminders handle the symptom. The terms you set before the project starts are the cure, and three of them do most of the work.

Take a deposit. A common structure is 50% upfront, before work begins, with the balance due on delivery. A client who has paid half is a client who finishes paying; the deposit also filters out clients who were never going to pay at all. For longer projects, split the fee into milestones — for example, 50% upfront, 25% at an agreed mid-project milestone, 25% on delivery — so you are never owed a large sum for finished work.

Shorten the terms. "Net 30" is a convention, not a law. Many freelancers invoice on Net 14 or Net 7 without pushback, simply because it was in the agreement from the start. Sample wording:

PAYMENT TERMS

Invoices are payable within fourteen (14) days of the invoice
date (Net 14), by the payment method stated on the invoice.
Work on subsequent project phases begins once payment for the
previous phase has been received.

Add a late fee clause. A late fee works mainly as a deterrent — it gives punctual payment a reason and overdue payment a cost. Sample wording:

LATE PAYMENT

Amounts not paid by the due date will incur a late fee of
[1.5%] of the outstanding amount per month [or: a fixed fee of
____ per overdue invoice], calculated from the due date until
payment is received in full. The Designer may pause work on any
project for the Client while an invoice remains unpaid beyond
its due date.

This is sample wording, not legal advice. Late fee rules and enforceability vary by country and state — in some places they are capped or regulated — so have a local lawyer review terms you rely on, especially for large contracts.

FAQ

What if the client says the payment is "being processed"?
Ask for two specifics: the date it will leave their account, and a remittance advice or payment reference once sent. "Being processed" with a date is a normal accounts-payable answer; "being processed" with no date, repeated across two reminders, is a stall. Log the promised date and follow up the day after it passes, referencing it.

Should I pause work as soon as an invoice is overdue?
Not usually at day 7 — pausing is leverage, and spending it on a likely oversight damages the relationship for nothing. Pause when the sequence reaches the final notice, or earlier if the overdue amount is large relative to the ongoing work, or the client stops replying. Whatever you decide, state it in writing before you act on it, as the day-30 template does.

Can I charge a late fee if my contract doesn't mention one?
In most cases, a late fee needs to have been agreed in advance to be enforceable — which is why the clause above belongs in the contract, not in the reminder email. Some jurisdictions give freelancers statutory rights to interest on late commercial payments, but the rules differ widely. If a large invoice is unpaid and your contract is silent, get local legal advice rather than adding a fee unilaterally.
