Client Ghosted Your Proposal? The 3-Email Follow-Up System

You did the call, scoped the work, and sent a careful proposal. Then nothing. No yes, no no, no reply at all — and no clear moment when you are supposed to stop waiting and move on.

This is not a rare edge case. In the Smallpdf Freelancer Freedom Index (2026, survey of 397 freelancers), 35% of freelancers said they had experienced clients stalling, delaying, or going silent at the contract and proposal stage. In the same survey, another 35% said they often cannot tell whether a client has even opened the contract or proposal they sent. The Leapers annual survey (1,000+ respondents, reported by Design Week) found a similar pattern from the other side of the timeline: 49% of freelancers said they had been ghosted by a client before work had even started.

Silence at this stage is expensive in a quiet way. You hold time in your schedule you cannot sell to anyone else, and hesitate to chase other work in case this project lands. The fix is not a cleverer single email. It is a short, pre-decided system: three emails at set intervals, each with a different job, and a clear point where you stop. This page gives you that system with complete templates you can copy and send.

Why proposals go silent

It helps to separate what silence usually means from what it feels like. It feels like rejection. In practice, the common causes are mundane: the proposal slipped down a busy inbox; your contact needs approval from someone else; the budget needs sign-off; or the client is comparing options and has not decided. Sometimes the project has genuinely died, and the client avoids sending bad news.

None of those causes are fixed by waiting longer. They are fixed, if at all, by making it easy to reply. Each email below lowers the effort of responding, restates the decision in its smallest form, and gives the client a graceful way to say no as well as yes. A clear no beats another two weeks of limbo, because it frees the time you were holding.

Decide the whole sequence before you send the first follow-up. Writing each nudge in the moment, while frustrated, produces emails that get longer and sharper as the silence stretches. Writing all three now, while you are calm, keeps the tone steady.

The system at a glance

The rhythm is simple. Send the proposal with a stated validity period and a named next step (more on that at the end of this article). If you hear nothing:

  • Day 3: a light check-in. Assume the proposal may not have been read yet. Restate the summary in a few lines so the client can decide without reopening the document.
  • Day 7: a decision-shaping email. Reduce the choice to two or three simple options and attach a real date — the proposal expiry or your next available start slot.
  • Day 14: the breakup email. Politely close the loop, release the held time, and leave the door open without leaving the proposal hanging.

After the third email, you stop. No fourth nudge, no “just bumping this to the top of your inbox” a month later. If the client comes back after that, treat it as a new enquiry: confirm scope and price are still valid before you restart anything.

Email 1 — Day 3: the light check-in

The job of this email is not to pressure. It is to confirm the proposal arrived, put the essentials back in front of the client in under a minute of reading, and make the next step obvious. Keep it short. If your day-3 email needs scrolling, it is working against you.

Subject: Quick check-in on the [Project Name] proposal

Hi [First Name],

Just checking the proposal I sent on [date] landed safely — here is the short version in case it is buried in your inbox:

- Scope: [one line, e.g. "Homepage redesign plus four landing pages, two revision rounds included"]
- Price: [total, and payment split, e.g. "$3,400 total — 50% to book, 50% on delivery"]
- Start: I can begin on [date] if we confirm this week

The full proposal is attached again for convenience. If it helps, I am happy to walk through it on a 15-minute call — or you can simply reply to this email and I will send the booking details.

Thanks,
[Your Name]
[Your business name / website]

Two details matter here. First, the recap: because 35% of freelancers in the Smallpdf survey cannot tell whether their proposal was opened at all, do not make your follow-up depend on the client finding the original document. Second, the closing offers two reply paths — a call or a one-line reply — so the client can pick the lower-effort one.

Email 2 — Day 7: narrow the choice, name a date

By day 7, “did you see it?” is no longer the useful question. The useful question is “what is the decision, and what is blocking it?” This email works by shrinking the decision. Instead of an open-ended “any thoughts?”, it offers a small set of options — proceed, adjust, or pass — each of which is a complete and acceptable answer. It also introduces a real deadline. Deadlines only work if they are true: use your proposal's expiry date or a start slot you genuinely cannot hold beyond that date.

Subject: [Project Name] — shall I hold your start date?

Hi [First Name],

Following up on the [Project Name] proposal from [date]. I currently have [start date] held for you, but I will need to release it after [expiry date] as I plan the next few weeks.

Whichever of these fits, a one-line reply is plenty:

1. Go ahead — please send the booking details and deposit invoice.
2. Interested, but something needs adjusting (price, scope, or timing) — tell me what, and I will revise the proposal.
3. Not proceeding / not right now — no problem at all, just let me know and I will close the file.

If the blocker is something else — approval, budget timing, another decision-maker — I am happy to send a shorter summary you can forward internally.

Thanks,
[Your Name]

Notice what option 2 does: it surfaces hidden blockers without accusing the client of anything. Much “ghosting” is actually an unresolved objection the client does not want to raise. Giving them a named way to raise it — price, scope, timing — gets you an answer you can act on.

Email 3 — Day 14: the breakup email

The final email is the one freelancers skip, and it is often the one that gets a reply. Its power comes from closure: you are withdrawing the held slot and closing the file, which turns an indefinite maybe into a now-or-later decision. Keep it warm and brief. No guilt, no sarcasm, no summary of how many times you have written. State what you are doing, when the proposal expires, and how easily the conversation can restart.

Subject: Closing the loop on [Project Name]

Hi [First Name],

I have not heard back on the [Project Name] proposal, so I will close the file for now and release the start date I was holding.

The proposal stays valid until [expiry date]; after that, pricing and availability may change, but I am glad to refresh it if the project comes back to life.

If the timing is simply wrong, feel free to reply whenever things are clearer — a short note is all it takes to restart.

Thanks for considering me, and good luck with [the launch / the rebrand / the project].

Best,
[Your Name]

Send this and mean it. Do not follow it with another check-in “just in case” — that teaches clients your deadlines are decorative.

Spacing and stopping rules

A few operating rules keep the system clean:

  • Count days loosely. Day 3, 7, and 14 are guides. If day 3 falls on a weekend, send on the next working day.
  • Reply in the same thread. One thread keeps the proposal history in one place, with your day-7 options directly under your day-3 summary.
  • One channel only. Do not chase the same silence across email, LinkedIn, and text at once. It reads as pressure, not professionalism.
  • Stop at three. The only exception is new information — a partial reply, or a date the client named has now passed (“we decide after the 20th” earns one follow-up on the 21st, not a restart of the sequence).
  • Log it and let go. Note the proposal, follow-up dates, and outcome in your admin system. If follow-ups, invoices, and intake are eating more hours than you realised, our freelance admin hours audit helps you find where the time goes.

Make silence less likely next time

Follow-ups treat the symptom. Three habits, built into the proposal itself, reduce how often you need them.

Put an expiry date on the proposal. “This proposal is valid until [date].” One line, near the price. It is not a pressure tactic — your availability and costs genuinely do change — and it gives both sides a shared, neutral deadline to work to. It also makes your day-7 and day-14 emails honest: you are not inventing urgency, you are referencing a date the client saw from the start.

Name the exact next action. End the proposal with the single step that starts the project: “To book, reply to this email and I will send the agreement and the deposit invoice. Work begins once the deposit clears.” Vague endings (“let me know what you think”) invite vague responses, which is to say, none. The clearer the next step, the more a follow-up can simply point back to it.

Ask the qualifying questions before you write the proposal. Budget range, decision-maker, and target start date are much easier to learn on a call than to guess afterwards. A structured set of questions — the kind we lay out in our design project intake form guide — filters out projects that were never going to proceed, which are the ones most likely to end in silence.

Use a sending method that confirms delivery and opening, where you can. The Smallpdf finding that 35% of freelancers cannot tell whether a proposal was opened points to a tooling gap, not a personal failure. Proposal and e-signature tools as a category exist partly to close that gap, with delivery and open notifications — so you know whether you are following up on an unread document or an undecided reader. Those situations deserve different emails. Follow up on facts, not guesses.

FAQ

What if the client replies weeks later, after the breakup email?
Welcome it, but restart cleanly. Confirm the scope is unchanged, check the price is still right for you, and issue a refreshed proposal with a new expiry date rather than reviving the old one. Do not apologise for having closed the file — releasing held time after two weeks of silence is normal professional practice.

Should I follow up faster for small projects?
You can compress the sequence for small, quick-turnaround work — day 2, day 5, day 10 is reasonable — because the client's own decision cycle is shorter. Keep the structure identical: check-in, options with a date, polite close. What you should not do is send more emails; three remains the limit.

Is it ever right to follow up by phone or message instead?
If you already have an established relationship and a standing channel — a regular call, a shared workspace — one brief note there alongside the day-7 email can be appropriate. For a new prospect you have only dealt with by email, stay in email. Switching channels to get around silence usually feels like escalation, and it rarely changes the answer.
