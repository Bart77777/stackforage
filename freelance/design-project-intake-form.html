The Design Intake Form: 21 Questions to Ask Before You Touch a Pixel

Most design projects do not stall during the design work. They stall before it — waiting for a logo file that turns out to be a screenshot in a Word document, copy that was never written, or photos that exist only on someone's phone. The work was booked, the deadline was set, and then nothing can start because the inputs were never collected.

Client silence makes this worse. According to the Leapers annual survey of more than 1,000 freelancers, reported by Design Week, 49% of freelancers have been ghosted by a client before work even started, and 27% have been ghosted after a project began. The same survey found poor communication was the top client-related stress factor. An intake form will not fix a client who disappears, but it does two useful things: it collects everything you need in one pass, while the client is still engaged and motivated, and it creates a written record of what was promised, by whom, and by when.

Below is a complete 21-question intake form for design projects. It is grouped so clients can complete it in sections, and every question includes a short note on why it is worth asking. Copy it into whatever form tool you already use, and send it as part of onboarding — before the start date, not after. Business & goals (4 questions)

  1. What is this project for, in one or two sentences?
    Why ask: A clear purpose statement stops you designing the wrong thing well. If the client cannot state it, that is useful to learn now, not at the first review.
  2. What should this design make people do? (Buy, sign up, call, visit, understand something, trust the brand more?)
    Why ask: Every layout decision gets easier when there is one primary action to design towards.
  3. How will you judge whether this project succeeded?
    Why ask: Success criteria agreed at the start protect you from vague feedback later — "it just doesn't feel right" is harder to argue with than a stated goal.
  4. What is the one thing this design must not do or must not look like?
    Why ask: Deal-breakers are cheap to respect when you know them upfront and expensive to discover after two rounds of concepts.

Audience (3 questions)

  1. Who is this for? Describe your typical customer or reader.
    Why ask: "Everyone" is not an audience. Age range, context, and what they already know about the client change tone, type size, and imagery.
  2. What do those people care about most when choosing a business like yours?
    Why ask: This tells you what to make prominent — price, speed, quality, local presence — instead of guessing.
  3. Where will people see this design first? (Phone, print, shop window, social feed, email?)
    Why ask: The first viewing context decides format priorities. A design that is mostly seen on phones should be checked on a phone first.

Content & assets (5 questions)

  1. Please upload your logo files. Which formats do you have? (Vector files such as AI, EPS, or SVG are best; PNG with a transparent background is the next best option.)
    Why ask: Asking about formats exposes the "we only have a JPG from an old email" problem immediately, while there is still time to recreate or request the proper files.
  2. Is the final text (copy) written? If yes, please paste it or attach it. If not, who is writing it, and by when?
    Why ask: Designing around placeholder text and then refitting real copy is one of the most common hidden time costs in design projects.
  3. Please upload the photos or images to be used, or confirm whether stock images or a photoshoot are needed.
    Why ask: Image supply affects the schedule and the budget. Low-resolution or unlicensed images found late become your emergency to solve.
  4. Do you have brand guidelines? If yes, please attach them. If not, list any required colours, fonts, or logo usage rules.
    Why ask: Guidelines arriving in week three can invalidate work done in weeks one and two. Even a short list of fixed colours and fonts is far better than none.
  5. Is there anything else that must appear in the design? (Legal text, disclaimers, certification marks, addresses, QR codes, social handles?)
    Why ask: Mandatory elements are easy to include from the start and painful to squeeze in at the end, especially in print layouts with no spare space.

Design preferences (3 questions)

  1. Share 2–3 examples of designs you like — from any industry — and say what you like about each.
    Why ask: Examples communicate taste faster than adjectives. "Clean and modern" means something different to every client; a reference image does not.
  2. Share 1–2 examples of designs you dislike, and say why.
    Why ask: Knowing what to avoid narrows the field as effectively as knowing what to aim for, and it prevents the most common first-review disappointment.
  3. Who are your main competitors, and should this design look similar to them or clearly different?
    Why ask: Some clients want to match their market; others want to stand apart from it. Designing against the wrong assumption wastes a full concept round.

Technical & access (3 questions)

  1. What are the exact technical requirements? (Finished sizes, file formats needed, print or screen, number of pages or versions?)
    Why ask: A wrong trim size or a missing format discovered at delivery means re-exporting everything — or worse, reprinting.
  2. Do you already have a domain, website, hosting, or social accounts this project connects to? Who manages them?
    Why ask: For web and social work, access is often the real bottleneck. Learning that a third party controls the domain on day one lets you request access in parallel instead of in sequence.
  3. If a printer, developer, or other supplier is involved, who are they, and have they provided specifications?
    Why ask: Printer and developer specifications (bleed, colour profile, image dimensions, file types) must shape the files from the start, not be checked at the end.

Timeline, decisions & payment (3 questions)

  1. Who gives feedback, and who gives final approval? Please name one main contact.
    Why ask: Feedback from several people with no single decision-maker is how projects loop. One named approver keeps decisions final. Clear approval rights also support the scope boundaries in our guide to scope creep contract clauses.
  2. What is the deadline, and is it tied to a fixed event? (Launch date, event, print slot, campaign start?)
    Why ask: A deadline tied to a booked event cannot move, which changes how you schedule revisions and what risks you flag early.
  3. When will you deliver the content and assets listed above, and do you agree that the project timeline starts when they are received?
    Why ask: This is the most important scheduling question on the form. It connects asset delivery to the timeline in writing, before goodwill turns into a dispute.

Put the asset deadline in the contract

A form answer is a statement of intent; a contract clause is what you point to when the assets are two weeks late and the deadline has not moved. Add wording along these lines to your agreement. This is sample wording, not legal advice — have a qualified professional review your contract for your situation.

Client materials and start of work

The project schedule begins when all materials listed in the intake
form (including logo files, final text, and images) have been
received from the Client.

If materials are not received by [date], the delivery date will move
by at least the same number of days as the delay, and the Designer
may reschedule the project behind other booked work.

The Designer will send one reminder if materials are outstanding.
Work will not begin on incomplete materials unless agreed in writing.

The clause works because it is mechanical rather than personal. You are not complaining about a late client; you are applying a rule both sides agreed to before the project started.

Turning the questions into a form

You do not need specialist software for this. Any general form tool that supports sections, required fields, and file uploads will do — tools in the online form builder category, such as Google Forms, are commonly used for intake forms. A few setup choices matter more than the tool itself:

  • Make the asset questions required, with file upload fields. A logo question that accepts only text will get text. Give clients a place to attach the actual files.
  • Keep the six group headings as form sections. A 21-question form feels long; six short sections feel manageable, and clients can pause between them.
  • Ask for formats explicitly. On the logo question, list the preferred formats (vector first, then transparent PNG) in the question text, so clients check what they have instead of sending the only file they can find.
  • Send the form before the kickoff call, and review answers on the call. Gaps and contradictions are much easier to resolve in conversation than over email, and the call confirms the named approver and deadline while everyone is present.
  • Save the submitted answers with the project files. The intake form becomes your reference when a later request conflicts with what was agreed at the start.

One caution: do not ask for passwords in a form. For account access, ask who manages the account (question 17) and arrange access separately through the service's own sharing or invitation features.

FAQ

Isn't 21 questions too many? Won't clients refuse to complete it?
Most clients complete a grouped form faster than they answer the same questions scattered across two weeks of emails — which is the real alternative. If a client pushes back, that is information too: a project with no agreed inputs, approver, or deadline is a project priced and scheduled on guesswork.

What if a client genuinely doesn't have some materials yet?
That is normal, and the form handles it: questions 9 and 21 ask who is producing the material and by when. What matters is that the dependency is named, dated, and connected to the timeline in writing, rather than discovered mid-project.

Should the intake form replace a kickoff call?
No — it makes the kickoff call shorter and more useful. Send the form first, then use the call to clarify unclear answers, confirm the decision-maker, and agree the asset delivery date. Decisions confirmed verbally on the call should be noted in a follow-up email so they are on record.
