Invoicing for Designers: Templates, Terms, and a Month-End Routine That Takes 20 Minutes

Invoicing is a small task that repeats forever: create the invoice, send it, check whether it was paid, record it, chase it if it was not. Done by hand for every client, every month, it becomes one of the steadiest drains in a freelance business — part of the wider admin load covered in our admin hours audit. This guide gives you a complete invoice template for design work, plain-English payment terms you can set before a project starts, and a month-end routine that keeps the whole loop to about 20 minutes.

Why payment delays are the default, not the exception

It helps to know what normal looks like, because most freelancers assume late payment is a personal failure. The surveys say otherwise.

According to the Smallpdf Freelancer Freedom Index 2026, a survey of 397 freelancers, 54% wait more than a week to be paid after sending an invoice, and 24% wait more than two weeks. The Leapers annual survey, with more than 1,000 respondents and reported by Design Week, found that 68% of freelancers have experienced late payment, and 43% have been offered payment terms of 60 days or more.

Two conclusions follow. If you invoice on vague terms and wait, delays are the expected outcome, not bad luck. And the parts you control — clear terms agreed in advance, a deposit, prompt invoices with everything a finance department needs — are worth setting up properly. They do not guarantee on-time payment, but they give you a clear date, a clear amount, and a paper trail, which is what makes a polite, firm follow-up possible.

The complete invoice template

An invoice has one job: to let the person paying it verify, approve, and pay it without asking you a single question. Every field below exists because its absence causes a delay. Copy the layout, replace the examples with your own details, and keep the structure identical for every client — consistency is what makes the month-end routine fast.

A note on tax: how tax applies to freelance design work — whether you must charge it, at what rate, and how it must be shown — differs by country, and sometimes by state or province, and by whether your client is a business or a consumer. This template shows tax as a separate line so you can include it correctly for your situation, but it is not tax advice. Check the rules that apply where you are registered, or ask an accountant once and then use the same treatment every time.

Payment terms, in plain English

Payment terms are simply the agreement about when an invoice must be paid, stated before work begins. You will see them written as "Net" plus a number.

Net 7 means the full amount is due within 7 days of the invoice issue date. Net 14 means due within 14 days. Net 30 means due within 30 days. The number is counted from the issue date on the invoice, not from when the client gets around to opening it — which is why the template above prints the actual due date as well as the Net term.

Which should a designer choose? Shorter terms suit small projects, final balances, and new clients. Net 14 is a common middle ground for ongoing relationships. Net 30 is standard for many larger companies with monthly payment runs — you may not be able to change it, but you should know it before agreeing to the project, because it affects cash flow, not just patience. Given that 43% of freelancers in the Leapers survey have been offered terms of 60 days or more, discuss terms at the proposal stage, not after an invoice is overdue. If a client requires long terms, that is a pricing and deposit conversation.

Whatever you choose, state in writing before the project starts: the term itself, what is invoiced at each stage (see deposits below), and whether final files transfer on final payment. Terms agreed in advance turn a chase email into a simple reference to an agreement.

Deposits and milestones: getting paid during the project

The single most effective structural change is to stop invoicing everything at the end. Two standard patterns work for design projects.

Deposit plus final balance. A deposit — a set percentage of the fee or a fixed amount — is invoiced and paid before work begins, with the balance invoiced on delivery. It confirms the booking and means a non-paying client costs you less. The exact percentage is your choice; what matters is that it is agreed in writing and received before you start.

Milestone payments. For larger projects, split the fee across agreed stages. One common structure is a deposit at booking, a payment at an approved mid-point such as sign-off on drafts, and the balance on final delivery — for example, 40% / 30% / 30%, or equal thirds on a longer project. Each milestone invoice references the stage that was approved, which keeps the client's approvals and your payments moving together. If a payment stalls, you pause at a natural boundary rather than mid-deliverable.

Keep the structure simple enough to explain in two lines in your proposal.

The 20-minute month-end routine

Run this once a month, on the same day, with your invoice list or a simple tracker open. It has five steps and should take about 20 minutes once your template and terms are set up.

Step 1 — Find unbilled work (5 minutes). Go through your active and recently completed projects. For each one ask: has every completed milestone been invoiced? Check delivered work against invoices sent. Unbilled completed work is the most common leak, and it is invisible until you look for it deliberately.

Step 2 — Issue the invoices (5 minutes). Create each missing invoice from your template, in sequence, with the next invoice numbers. Because the layout never changes, this is filling in fields, not designing documents. Send them the same day — an invoice sitting in your drafts is earning nothing, and remember that in the Smallpdf survey 54% of freelancers wait more than a week after invoicing to be paid, so every day you delay sending adds directly to that wait.

Step 3 — Match payments received (4 minutes). Check your bank or payment account for the month and mark each invoice as paid, partially paid, or unpaid. Note the date paid next to each invoice number. If a payment does not match an invoice amount, flag it now rather than at tax time.

Step 4 — Chase what is overdue (4 minutes). For every unpaid invoice past its due date, send a reminder the same day. Do not rewrite these each month — use a short, polite sequence that escalates gradually. Our late-payment email templates give you ready-to-send wording for the first reminder through to the firmer follow-ups, so this step is copy, adjust the dates, and send.

Step 5 — Record and file (2 minutes). Save each invoice and its payment status in one place, in invoice-number order. Update your income record for the month. Then stop. The routine works because it is bounded: five steps, once a month, no open loops carried in your head between runs.

If the routine regularly takes much longer than 20 minutes, the cause is usually invoice details collected at invoicing time instead of at project start, terms that were never agreed, or records kept in several places. Fix the cause in your template or intake process, not by spending longer on the routine.

FAQ

What should I do if a client asks for Net 60 terms?
Treat it as a business decision, not an admin detail. In the Leapers survey, 43% of freelancers have been offered terms of 60 days or more, so you will likely face this. You can accept with a larger deposit, price the wait into the fee, split the project into milestones so part of the fee arrives earlier, or decline. What you should not do is accept silently and discover the cash-flow gap two months later.

Do I need invoicing software, or is a template enough?
A template is enough when your invoice volume is low and your routine is consistent — the system in this article runs on a document template and a list. Software in the invoicing and accounting category becomes useful when volume grows, when you want automatic reminders, or when payment links would remove friction for your clients. Either way, the template fields, terms, and month-end routine stay the same; the tool only changes how much of it is typed by hand.

When exactly should I send the final invoice?
On delivery, the same day, unless your agreement specifies a milestone schedule that places it elsewhere. The payment clock in most terms starts at the issue date, and survey data shows payment commonly takes a week or more after that. Sending promptly is the one part of the timeline entirely in your control.
