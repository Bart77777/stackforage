The 3-Column Project Tracker: Manage Four Clients Without Project Software

If you are holding three or four client projects in your head right now, you are not disorganised. You are average.

According to the Squarespace Circle State of the Web Design Industry 2024 survey of 1,000 creative professionals, conducted with OnePoll, web designers handle an average of 3.26 projects a month. In the same survey, 27% named time management as one of their biggest challenges.

That combination is the problem. Three projects is too many to track from memory, especially when each one is waiting on a different person. But it is not enough work to justify spending your Friday afternoon maintaining a complex project board.

This guide gives you a middle option: a single spreadsheet with three status values — with me, with client, done — and a 15-minute weekly routine that keeps it accurate. No new software to learn, no subscription, and nothing to configure beyond one header row you can copy and paste below.

Why project management tools often do not stick for solo freelancers

It is worth being honest about this, because the usual advice — “just use a proper tool” — misses the real issue.

Most project management tools are not badly designed. Many are excellent for teams. The problem for a solo freelancer is update cost.

A team tool assumes someone will create tasks, assign owners, set dependencies, add subtasks, update percentages, and close tickets. In a team, that overhead is shared and the shared view is worth it. When you work alone, you pay all of the overhead yourself, and you already know most of what the board is trying to tell you — except for the one thing you actually forget: who is waiting on whom, and for how long.

So the board drifts. You update it in a burst on Monday, skip it on a busy Wednesday, and by the following week you no longer trust it. Once you stop trusting it, you stop opening it. The tool did not fail. The routine was simply too expensive to maintain for one person juggling about three projects.

A spreadsheet tracker works differently. It asks for far less: one row per project, one status choice from three options, and a date for when you last touched it. That is information you can update in under a minute at the end of a work session. Low cost is the whole design.

If your admin time feels scattered in other ways too — invoicing, formatting documents, chasing files — it helps to see the full picture first. The idea: three statuses, nothing else

Every active project, at any moment, is in one of three states:

  • With me — the next step is yours. Designing, revising, exporting, writing the email.
  • With client — the next step is theirs. You are waiting for feedback, content, approval, or payment before you can sensibly continue.
  • Done — delivered and closed. Keep the row for reference, but it no longer needs your attention.

That is deliberately crude. It does not capture “70% complete” or “phase 2 of 4.” It captures the only question that prevents dropped balls: whose move is it?

When a project is “with client” for too long, you know to follow up. When too many projects are “with me” at once, you know your week is overloaded before the deadlines tell you. When everything is “with client,” you know you have a quiet window — and possibly a cash-flow gap coming.

The full tracker: every column explained

The tracker has eight columns. Each one earns its place by answering a question you will actually ask during the week.

ClientClient name or codeGroups your work by who it is for, at a glance.
ProjectShort project name, e.g. “Homepage redesign”One row is one project, not one task.
StatusWith me / With client / Done — only these threeShows who holds the next move.

Last touchedDate you last worked on it or contacted the client about itYour early-warning system. See the rule below.
Fee & payment statusAgreed fee and a short note: “Deposit paid,” “Final invoice sent”Keeps money visible next to work, so finished projects do not sit uninvoiced.

Notice what is not here: no priority scores, no percentage complete, no time estimates per task. You can add columns later if a real need appears. Start with eight.

Copy-paste header row

Copy this header row into the first row of a new spreadsheet:

Client	Project	Status	Deadline	Waiting on	Next action	Last touched	Fee & payment status

The columns are separated by tabs, so pasting into Google Sheets or Excel should split them into separate columns automatically.

How to build it in Google Sheets or Excel

Step 1: Create a blank sheet. In Google Sheets, start a new spreadsheet and name it something boring and clear, like “Project Tracker 2026.” In Excel, create a new workbook and rename the first sheet “Tracker.” Boring names get found again in three months.

Step 2: Paste the header row. Click cell A1, paste the header row above, and bold it. Freeze the top row (in Sheets: View > Freeze > 1 row; in Excel: View > Freeze Panes > Freeze Top Row) so the headers stay visible as your list grows.

Step 3: Limit the Status column to three choices. In Sheets, select the Status column, then use Data > Data validation to create a dropdown with exactly: With me, With client, Done. In Excel, use Data > Data Validation > List with the same three values. This step matters more than it looks. A dropdown takes one click and prevents the slow creep toward statuses like “almost done,” “waiting-ish,” and “in progress??” that make a tracker unreadable.

Step 4: Add one row per current project. Go through your inbox and recent files and add every live project, even the quiet ones. Especially the quiet ones — they are the ones this system exists to catch. Fill in Status, Deadline, and Last touched as honestly as you can. Leave “Waiting on” blank unless you are genuinely blocked.

Step 5: Sort or filter by Status. Both Sheets and Excel let you filter the Status column. Filtering to “With me” gives you your real to-do list. Filtering to “With client” gives you your follow-up list. That is the tracker doing its job.

Step 6 (optional): Add simple colour. If you like visual cues, use conditional formatting to shade “With client” rows lightly once Last touched is more than seven days old. Keep it to one rule. A tracker that needs a formatting manual will not survive a busy month.

The three operating rules

A template without rules becomes another abandoned file. These three rules are the system.

Rule 1: Statuses stay at three. The moment you add “in review,” “on hold,” and “nearly there,” you are back to interpreting instead of scanning. A held project is still either waiting on you or waiting on the client. Pick the honest one and use the “Waiting on” column for the detail.

Rule 2: Review it once a week for 15 minutes. Pick a fixed slot — Friday afternoon and Monday morning both work well — and do the same short routine every time: update every Status, refresh every Last touched date that changed, rewrite any stale Next action, and check deadlines for the next two weeks. Fifteen minutes is enough for three to five projects, and it is short enough that you will actually do it. Skipping a week is fine occasionally; the review is also how you recover, because rebuilding the picture is the review.

Rule 3: If Last touched is more than 7 days old, make contact. For a “With client” row, that means a brief, friendly follow-up naming the specific thing you are waiting on — the “Waiting on” column writes that email for you. For a “With me” row, it means the project has quietly slipped: either schedule a real work block for it or tell the client honestly when you will pick it up. Silence is what turns a small delay into a problem; a short message at day seven prevents most of it.

What this tracker will not do

It will not break a project into tasks, track your hours, send reminders automatically, or show a client a polished portal. Those are real needs for some freelancers, and dedicated tools handle them better.

What it does is narrower: it keeps the state of every project visible in one place, in a format cheap enough to maintain. For pricing in particular, you will need hours as well as status. FAQ

How many projects can this handle before it breaks down?
Comfortably, up to about eight to ten active rows. That covers the typical load suggested by the Squarespace Circle survey average of 3.26 projects a month, with room for overlap between months. If you regularly run far more concurrent projects than that, or you coordinate other people, a dedicated project tool will serve you better — the update-cost trade-off changes when a team shares the board.

Should I track tasks inside each project as well?
Not in this sheet. Keep one row per project and let the “Next action” column hold the single next step. If a project genuinely needs a task list, keep that list in whatever notes or design file you already use for the work itself. Mixing project status and task detail in one sheet is how trackers become heavy and stop being updated.

What do I do with Done rows?
Leave them in place until payment has cleared, since the Fee & payment status column is your reminder. After that, move them to a second sheet tab called “Archive” during your weekly review. Keeping the main tab to live work preserves the ten-second scan, and the archive becomes a useful record of past fees when you price similar work later.
