Your Project Rate Is a Guess: Track One Project Honestly and Price the Next One Right

Project pricing is the norm for design work. Guessing the hours behind it is also the norm — and that is where the money leaks.

According to the Squarespace Circle State of the Web Design Industry 2024 survey of 1,000 creative professionals, conducted with OnePoll, 77% of designers prefer to charge by the project rather than by the hour. In the same survey, 36% named pricing as one of their biggest challenges — the second most common challenge reported, after finding new clients.

Those two numbers fit together. A fixed project fee is good for clients: it is predictable and easy to approve. But a fixed fee is only good for you if the number of hours inside it is roughly right. If you never measure the hours, every quote is a fresh guess, and the guesses tend to be optimistic.

You do not need to track every job forever to fix this. You need one honestly tracked project, a simple formula, and a worksheet you reuse. This guide walks through all three.

The worked example: quoted for 20 hours, actually took 45

The example below is illustrative — the fee, the estimate, and the hours are example numbers chosen to make the maths easy to follow, not figures from a survey. The pattern it shows, though, is the one worth checking in your own work.

Suppose you quote a project at a flat fee, expecting it to take about 20 hours. Suppose your target hourly rate — the rate you intend to earn — is $75 an hour.

What you expected:

  • Planned hours: 20
  • Fee implied by your target rate: 20 × $75 = $1,500
  • So you quote $1,500.

What actually happened:

  • Actual hours, including revisions, calls, file prep, and admin for the project: 45
  • Fee received: still $1,500 — it was a fixed price.

Your effective hourly rate:

Effective hourly rate = fee ÷ actual hours

$1,500 ÷ 45 hours = $33.33 per hour

Compare that with the $75 you intended. $33.33 is about 44% of $75 — less than half. Another way to see it: the extra 25 hours were effectively unpaid. At your target rate, 25 hours × $75 = $1,875 of work that the fee never covered. The project did not just run a little long; it ran to 2.25 times the estimate (45 ÷ 20 = 2.25).

None of this means the client did anything wrong. The fee was agreed in advance. It means the estimate was wrong, and without tracked hours you would never know by how much — you would only know the project “felt long,” and you would quote the next similar project the same way.

Step one: track one project honestly

Pick your next project — ideally a typical one, not your simplest or your most chaotic — and log the time on it from start to finish. One project is enough to start, because one real number is worth more than a year of estimates.

“Honestly” has a specific meaning here. Log all of the time the project causes, not just design time:

  • Calls, meetings, and reading long email threads
  • Design and production work
  • Revisions, including the small “quick tweaks”
  • Exporting, file preparation, and delivery
  • Project admin: proposals, invoicing, and follow-ups for that job

Log in short entries as you go, or in one pass at the end of each day while the work is still fresh. Do not reconstruct the whole project at the end — memory rounds down. Round each entry to the nearest 15 minutes; precision beyond that adds effort without improving the quote.

You can do this in a notebook, a notes app, a simple spreadsheet, or a time-tracking tool — the category of tool matters less than the habit of writing the entry the same day. What matters is the format: one line per work session, with the date, what you did, and how long it took.

A work log template you can copy

Project: [Project name] — Client: [Client]
Fee quoted: [Amount]   Estimated hours: [Number]

Date       | Work                                              | Hours
-----------|---------------------------------------------------|-------
2026-__-__ | Kickoff call + notes                              | 0.75
2026-__-__ | First concepts                                    | 3.50
2026-__-__ | Revision round 1 (client feedback)                | 1.25
2026-__-__ | Export, file prep, delivery                       | 0.50
           | TOTAL                                             |

At the end of the project, total the hours and calculate your effective hourly rate with the same formula as the example: fee ÷ actual hours. Then write down one sentence about where the time went that you did not expect — revisions, waiting-and-restarting, content chasing, extra concepts. That sentence is what improves the next estimate, because it tells you which part to pad, not just that you should pad.

Step two: price the next project with the formula

Once you have real hours from a comparable project, quote the next one like this:

Price = target hourly rate × expected hours × (1 + revision buffer) + external costs

Take each part in turn.

1. Target hourly rate. This is the rate you need and intend to earn per working hour. If you have never set one deliberately, a starting approach is to work backwards: take the annual income you need from design work, add your business costs and tax provision, and divide by the number of billable hours you can realistically work in a year — which is always fewer than your total working hours, since admin, marketing, and gaps between projects are unbilled. The exact figure is personal; what matters is that it is a chosen number, not whatever a fee happens to divide into.

2. Expected hours. Use the actual hours from your tracked project, adjusted for how the new project differs. Bigger scope than the tracked job? Scale up. Same client, same deliverables, content supplied up front this time? Your tracked figure may already be fair. The point is that you are now adjusting from evidence instead of estimating from optimism.

3. Revision buffer. Multiply by (1 + buffer rate). A buffer of 0.20 means you add 20% to the hours to cover revision rounds, feedback cycles, and the small requests that arrive on almost every job. See the guidance on choosing a rate below.

4. External costs. Add, at cost or with your usual handling approach, anything the project makes you buy: stock assets, fonts, specialist subcontracting, printing, or paid tools needed only for that job. Keeping these outside the hourly calculation stops them quietly eating your rate.

The formula on the example numbers

Using the earlier illustrative project as the tracked baseline — 45 actual hours — suppose your next similar project looks like this:

  • Target hourly rate: $75
  • Expected hours, based on the tracked job: 45
  • Revision buffer: 20%, so (1 + 0.20) = 1.20
  • External costs: $120 in stock assets

Price = $75 × 45 × 1.20 + $120
$75 × 45 = $3,375
$3,375 × 1.20 = $4,050
$4,050 + $120 = $4,170

That is a very different quote from $1,500 — and that is the point. The tracked project showed what this kind of work actually costs to deliver. If a client cannot meet a price built from real hours, that is a scope conversation to have before work starts: fewer concepts, fewer revision rounds, or a phased delivery. It is a far better conversation than discovering the gap alone, afterwards, in your effective hourly rate.

Choosing a revision buffer: guidelines, not rules

There is no universal correct buffer. Treat the following as starting guidelines to adjust against your own tracked projects, not as industry standards:

  • New client, unclear brief: consider a higher buffer, around 25–30%. You have no shared history, feedback style is unknown, and briefs tend to expand as the client sees real work.
  • Known client, similar repeat work: a lower buffer, around 10–15%, is often reasonable. You know how their feedback arrives and how many rounds they typically use.
  • Fixed, tightly specified deliverable — for example, production work from approved designs: the buffer can be smaller still, because the main source of extra hours, open-ended revision, is limited by the spec.
  • Work with many approvers: lean higher regardless of familiarity. Each additional reviewer tends to add a feedback cycle, and cycles are where hours hide.

Whatever you choose, write the buffer into your worksheet, not just the final price. After two or three tracked projects you can compare buffer against reality and tune it. That small feedback loop is the entire method.

Checklist: carrying tracked hours into your next quote

  • ☐ Previous similar project tracked, with total hours and effective hourly rate calculated
  • ☐ One sentence written on where the unexpected hours went
  • ☐ Target hourly rate set deliberately, and used consistently
  • ☐ Expected hours for the new quote based on tracked hours, adjusted for scope differences
  • ☐ Revision buffer chosen for this client and brief, and recorded in the worksheet
  • ☐ External costs listed separately and added on top
  • ☐ Included revision rounds stated in the quote itself, so the buffer and the terms agree
  • ☐ New project added to your tracker, ready to log from day one

FAQ

Do I need to track my time forever once I start?
No. The method here is to track one project honestly, then spot-check with another tracked project every few months or whenever your work changes type. Continuous tracking gives more data, and some designers prefer it, but the largest gain comes from the first real measurement — replacing a guess with a number. After that, periodic checks keep your pricing honest without turning logging into a second job.

Should I show clients my hourly calculation?
You can keep quoting a single project price — remember, 77% of designers in the Squarespace Circle survey prefer project pricing, and clients generally like the predictability. The hourly maths is your internal worksheet for arriving at that price. What is worth showing the client is the scope the price covers: deliverables, revision rounds, and what happens if the scope grows. That protects the price without turning the quote into a timesheet.

What if tracking shows my current prices are far too low?
Raise prices on new quotes first, using the formula, rather than trying to renegotiate work already agreed. Expect the tracked number to change how you talk about scope as much as how you price: smaller included revision rounds, clearer deliverables, and phased options all become natural once you can see what the hours cost. One tracked project will not fix every past quote, but it stops the next one repeating them.
